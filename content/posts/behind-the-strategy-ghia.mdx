Mélanie Masarin spent her childhood summers near the Mediterranean, where aperitivo is a way of life, and on Ghia's own site she says she wants to "take back the word drinking from alcohol." That line explains Ghia's marketing strategy better than any sober-curious label. The non-alcoholic aperitif sells the hour before dinner to people who still drink, and its smartest money went to restaurants that barely paid it back.

**The short version**

- Ghia markets to drinkers who want a night off, because about 92 percent of its buyers identify as alcohol consumers.
- It sells an occasion, the 6 p.m. ritual, and then sells the glass, the ice bucket, and the cookbook that fill it.
- The $38 price holds because the reason fits in one sentence: the ingredients cost more when there is no alcohol to preserve them.
- In 2022, restaurants took up to 40 percent of Ghia's marketing resources for about 4 percent of revenue. That trade is the part worth copying, and the part that most needs a number.

## What gap did Ghia's founder see?

Ghia started with a memory. On the brand's [Our Story page](https://drinkghia.com/pages/our-story), Masarin writes about her mother and grandmother making giant jugs of limoncello from freshly picked lemons, and about what she loved most in those nights: being together, letting loose, and laughing with friends. She wanted a drink that could take people to that place "without numbing the night," one "you'd remember in the morning."

The gap she saw was an hour with nothing good to pour. Masarin told [Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/blog/ghia-emerging-category) in 2023 that she built Ghia for specific moments, like "the drinks at the bar before dinner" and a friend's dinner party, and the Our Story page calls the drink an invitation "to move from work into play." Alcohol usually owns those hours, so the product had to look and taste like it belonged at the bar.

Image: Ghia." ratio="3/4" />

That starting point shaped everything after it. Because Ghia began with a scene instead of a health claim, every later choice could be tested against one question: does this belong at 6 p.m.?

## Who is Ghia's target customer?

It's tempting to file Ghia under sober-curious, the shelf of drinks for people cutting back or quitting. Masarin's own numbers put it somewhere else. In the [Hannah's List interview](https://hannahslist.substack.com/p/q-and-a-melanie-masarin-of-ghia) from August 2026, she said about 92 percent of the people who buy Ghia identify as alcohol consumers. She described them as people who want "great wine on a Saturday and something non-alcoholic on a Tuesday," and she said plainly that Ghia is "not a cessation product." As she put it, "Positioning Ghia against alcohol would have alienated most of the people we made it for."

She expects that habit to grow. Masarin told [The Daily Pour](https://thedailypour.com/no-low-alcohol/melanie-masarin-ghia-interview/) in March 2026 that "people will alternate more between alcoholic and non-alcoholic options," which means Ghia's growth sits with drinkers who switch on some nights. A brand built for that customer has no reason to talk about streaks or willpower, so Ghia talks about a good time.

Ghia's Instagram post from January 22, 2026, shows the approach in practice. In the month built around Dry January, the brand's note on the post simply waves at anyone "new here," with no mention of quitting.

<Instagram url="https://www.instagram.com/p/DT0pxDyFRFx/" title="Ghia on Instagram, January 2026" />

The lesson reaches past drinks. If your product gets filed under a niche, check who actually buys it before you choose an enemy, because the enemy you pick decides who feels welcome.

## Why is Ghia so expensive?

The product itself is where the price argument starts. A bottle of the Original Apéritif cost $38 in 2023, and it still lists at $38 on Ghia's site. Masarin told Shopify the brand got pushback because people felt a drink without alcohol should not cost the same as one with it. Her answer was the ingredients: without alcohol to preserve them, she estimated that what goes into a bottle of Ghia costs six to 10 times more than the ingredients in a comparable alcoholic drink.

<Figure src="/images/blog/behind-the-strategy-ghia/ghia-original-aperitif-ingredients.jpg" alt="A Ghia Original Apéritif bottle embossed with the words clear-eyed, beside a list of ingredients: white grape, yuzu, lemon balm, elderflower, gentian root, plum, rhubarb root, ginger, and rosemary" caption="Ghia puts the ingredient list next to the bottle on its product page, which turns the price argument into something you can read. Image: Ghia." ratio="1/1" />

Ghia answered the pushback with customer education and kept the price. That fits what Masarin told [Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/retailers/unapologetically-loud-and-fun-ghias-melanie-masarin-on-the-sober-curious-movement/) in January 2022: she never wanted "a lesser version of an alcoholic brand," and a cheaper bottle would have said exactly that. A premium holds when a buyer can repeat the reason in one sentence, and Ghia's reason (natural ingredients cost more without alcohol to keep them stable) fits in one.

## Ghia sells the 6 p.m. ritual, and its shop proves it

Because its buyers already drink, Ghia has no need to sell a replacement for alcohol, so it sells the moment alcohol usually fills. By 2026, Masarin was calling that moment "the 6pm ritual" in the Hannah's List interview, and the brand designed the objects that go with it. Shopify reported in 2023 that Ghia created its own glass, a stemless martini glass with a totem foot, and that it became one of the brand's big sellers. Ghia's site now sells them as Totem Glasses, a handblown set of two designed with Sophie Lou Jacobsen, for $110.

<Figure src="/images/blog/behind-the-strategy-ghia/ghia-totem-glasses-palma.jpg" alt="Two handblown Ghia Totem glasses filled with a red drink and ice, one with a green bulb foot and one with an amber bulb and green base" caption="The Totem glass gives the ritual a signature object, and every colorway makes a new photo. Image: Ghia." ratio="1/1" />

The rest of the shop follows the same logic. Ghia sells an ice bucket, a cocktail box with a bottle, a pour spout, and a Totem glass, and the Riviera Kitchen Set, which pairs a bottle of the aperitif with Riviera, the cookbook Masarin published in April 2026. According to Ghia's product page, Riviera collects 101 recipes from the coast of France and Italy, guided by her grandmother's kitchen rules.

<Figure src="/images/blog/behind-the-strategy-ghia/ghia-riviera-cookbook-kitchen-set.jpg" alt="A bottle of Ghia Original Apéritif standing beside the Riviera cookbook by Mélanie Masarin, its cream cover showing a fish on a platter" caption="The kitchen set sells dinner along with the drink, which moves Ghia from the bar cart to the table. Image: Ghia." ratio="1/1" />

The voice does the same job in fewer words. Ghia's newsletter sign-up reads "We're booze free, but our emails are intoxicating," and the bottle's shoulder is embossed with "clear-eyed." Both lines admit there is no alcohol and then move straight to the fun. An occasion gives a brand more to sell and more to post about than a category does, and none of these products has to mention what is missing.

## Why did Ghia spend on restaurants that barely paid?

Ghia was supposed to start in restaurants. Masarin told Hannah's List the plan was to launch on April 1, 2020, in about 50 restaurants only, and every restaurant closed two weeks before, so Ghia launched online. By January 2022, she told Modern Retail, 75 percent of the business was still online and Ghia was on more than 100 menus in America. Even so, Ghia was spending about 30 to 40 percent of its marketing resources on chefs, sampling, and tastings. That work brought in about 4 percent of revenue, and she called it "very much a long-term play."

<Bars caption="Ghia's restaurant channel, January 2022" source="Modern Retail interview with Melanie Masarin, January 2022." items={[{label: "Marketing resources, top of the 30 to 40 percent range", value: 40, display: "up to 40%", highlight: true}, {label: "Share of revenue", value: 4, display: "about 4%"}]} />

Four years later she still defends the trade. She told Hannah's List that getting into bars and restaurants "isn't the highest-ROI thing we do" from a pure sales and volume view, but those rooms are where Ghia builds affinity with customers and gets discovered. Her reasoning goes back to the customer: if people can only enjoy a non-alcoholic night at home on the couch, the brand hasn't finished its job. She still DMs restaurants and brings samples when she eats out.

The menu is where the positioning becomes visible. Masarin described the goal as a drink you can pour at aperitivo hour "next to someone's Negroni without anyone blinking," and a restaurant is the one place a drinker sees that scene for real.

<Figure src="/images/blog/behind-the-strategy-ghia/ghia-original-aperitif-vs-negroni.jpg" alt="Ghia graphic comparing the Original Apéritif with a Negroni: 0 percent versus 24 percent alcohol by volume, 3 grams versus 10 grams of sugar, and 20 versus 190 calories" caption="Ghia's own side-by-side sets the aperitif next to a Negroni, the same pairing Masarin wants to see on a bar. Image: Ghia." ratio="1/1" />

The spend that looks wasteful on a revenue chart is the channel doing the positioning work. A shelf can tell people Ghia exists, but only a bar shows them a drinker ordering it.

## What happened to Ghia after Shark Tank?

Masarin pitched Ghia in Season 14 of Shark Tank and asked for $250,000 for 5 percent. Kevin O'Leary countered at $250,000 for 10 percent, and she walked away without a deal, according to a July 2026 [Takeout recap](https://www.thetakeout.com/1872877/what-happened-shark-tank-ghia/). After the show, Ghia went into Whole Foods and Wegmans and later Target, The Takeout reported.

Ghia's own post from September 22, 2026, tags Whole Foods and asks who says you can't "color hunt" there. Even the grocery post sells color and mood, and it never mentions quitting.

<Instagram url="https://www.instagram.com/p/DdmMgSAlbRh/" title="Ghia at Whole Foods, September 2026" />

The shelf also changed what Ghia sells. Its site now carries Le Spritz, canned spritzes in flavors such as Lime & Salt, Ghia-Ginger, and Sumac & Chili, and the ice bucket is described as fitting ten Ghia cans. A can is easier to grab off a grocery shelf than a $38 bottle, and the colors keep it recognizable from across an aisle.

<Figure src="/images/blog/behind-the-strategy-ghia/ghia-le-spritz-cans.jpg" alt="Five Ghia Le Spritz cans stacked on a black background, in green, blue, red, burgundy, and pale yellow, each with the Ghia script logo" caption="Each Le Spritz flavor gets its own color but keeps the same slanted Ghia script, so the cans read as one family on a shelf. Image: Ghia." ratio="1/1" />

Ghia is still in business, and it is growing more carefully. Masarin told Hannah's List that two years ago the company decided to take the business to profitability, which meant downsizing the team and being more selective about growth. "Most of my job now is saying no to protect the business," she said. Saying no still leaves room for restaurants, because she told The Daily Pour she thinks a lot about "where and how people meet the brand."

## What to take from Ghia's marketing strategy

1. **Market to the buyers you have.** Ghia's buyers mostly drink, so it never picked alcohol as the enemy. Check who buys before you pick yours.
2. **Start from a scene you can describe.** Masarin's founding story is a table and an hour, which gives every later product a test it has to pass.
3. **Sell the occasion, then sell what fills it.** Ghia named the 6 p.m. ritual and built a glass, an ice bucket, and a cookbook kit around it.
4. **Give the price a reason people can repeat.** Ghia points to ingredients that cost six to 10 times more, and its product page lists them.
5. **Fund one channel for memory.** The restaurant spend buys the scene a shelf can't show, which is a stranger ordering your drink in public.

Graza had the same instinct in [olive oil](/blog/behind-the-strategy-graza), and Fishwife had it in [tinned fish](/blog/fishwife-marketing-strategy). For another drinks comparison, see how [Liquid Death and Poppi](/blog/liquid-death-vs-poppi-marketing) built their brands.

## The catch: the restaurant bet still needs a number

The easy read on Ghia is a pretty bottle that rode the sober-curious wave, and I think that misses the strategy. The opposite read is just as easy, though: that every brand should copy the restaurant spend because a smart founder defends it. I'd keep the spend. What I'd change is how it gets measured, because Masarin's defense rests on affinity and discovery, and neither one shows up on the revenue chart where restaurants earned about 4 percent.

That gap matters more now. A company that has chosen profitability and spends most of its founder's time "saying no" will eventually have to defend every line of the budget, and a channel argued on feel is the easiest one to cut. Athletic Brewing shows one way to give it a number. For its 2026 Athletic January, the brand added an OpenTable map of bars and restaurants serving its beer and a $5 rebate on one can or draft pour, [Beverage Industry reported](https://www.bevindustry.com/articles/98051-athletic-brewing-co-relaunches-athletic-january-campaign) in December 2025. A redeemed rebate is a trial you can count.

For Ghia, I would track the share of new customers who buy again within 90 days, split by whether they first met the brand in a restaurant or on a shelf. If the restaurant group comes back more often, that 4 percent of revenue starts to look cheap, and the channel has a number to stand on when the next budget cut comes.

Before your next launch, write down two things: the hour your product belongs to, and the first place a stranger will see someone else enjoying it. If the only answer to the second is your own feed, that is the channel to fund next, and the first thing to measure.

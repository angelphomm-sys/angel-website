Liquid Death vs. Poppi comes down to what each brand asks you to believe. Poppi sold a gut health promise you had to take on trust, while Liquid Death sold attitude plus numbers you can check on the label. The promise grew faster, but the facts have aged better, and Poppi's own recent marketing shows it.

**The short version**

- Poppi built a $1.95 billion brand on soda that was better for your gut, then paid $8.9 million to settle a lawsuit over that claim.
- Liquid Death never said its water does anything special. Its claims are numbers like calories and caffeine, and its edge is a voice a rival can't copy.
- In 2026, Poppi is selling mood, sports, and culture, which is closer to Liquid Death's playbook than to its own origin story.

## The toilet ad that put them on the same shelf

In May 2025, Liquid Death sat people on toilets arranged like a tasting room and had them compare its 10-calorie sparkling water with high-fiber prebiotic sodas, the category Poppi made famous. The tagline was "More Pop. Less Poop," [DesignRush reported](https://news.designrush.com/liquid-death-toilet-humor-war-against-high-fiber-sodas). The ad never named Poppi, and it didn't need to, because anyone who had walked down a soda aisle that year knew who it meant.

The timing explains the jab. Liquid Death had announced soda flavors like Killer Cola in January 2025, which moved it onto the same shelf as the prebiotic brands, [Modern Retail reported](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/how-liquid-death-is-tackling-the-soda-wars/) that March. Earlier in May, PepsiCo closed its $1.95 billion purchase of Poppi, according to [PepsiCo](https://www.pepsico.com/newsroom/press-releases/2025/pepsico-completes-acquisition-of-poppi-accelerating-strategic-portfolio-transformation). Two brands that started in different aisles were now asking the same shopper for the same dollar, and each was selling something very different to get it.

## Poppi's marketing strategy sold a promise

Poppi's pitch is that soda can feel good again. Its [website](https://drinkpoppi.com/) still leads with "soda's back, but better," and each can has no more than five grams of sugar, according to PepsiCo. The early marketing went further than lower sugar, though. Slogans like "gut health never tasted this good" and "for a healthy gut" told shoppers the drink would do something for their bodies, [Brewbound reported](https://www.brewbound.com/news/poppi-reaches-8-9m-settlement-over-gut-health-claims) in 2025.

The founder carried that promise to its first big audience. In 2021, co-founder Allison Ellsworth posted a TikTok telling the brand's story, went to bed, and woke up to more than $100,000 in Amazon sales, she told [Tasting Table](https://www.tastingtable.com/1912227/poppi-soda-move-boost-sales/). Founder video works because the person making the promise is a person you can see, which is the same reason [Bloom Nutrition's first buyers](/blog/bloom-nutrition-marketing-strategy) came from its co-founder's own following.

What customers bought was permission to drink soda without the guilt. That permission was a strong reason to try the can, but it rested on a health benefit the brand would have to keep proving.

## Why was Poppi sued over its gut health claims?

In May 2024, customers filed a class action arguing that the agave inulin in each can was far too little fiber to make a meaningful difference to gut health. In March 2025, the plaintiffs asked a federal judge to approve an $8.9 million settlement, and Poppi said it acknowledged "no fault, liability, or wrongdoing," Brewbound reported. The settlement ended the case, but it also put the brand's core promise on the record as something customers had challenged.

The lawsuit was only half the problem, because an ingredient is easy to copy. Coca-Cola announced Simply Pop with 6 grams of prebiotic fiber per can in February 2025, [TODAY reported](https://www.today.com/today/amp/rcna192666). In July 2025, PepsiCo, Poppi's new owner, announced Pepsi Prebiotic Cola with 3 grams of prebiotic fiber per can, [BevNET reported](https://www.bevnet.com/news/2025/pepsi-announces-new-prebiotic-cola-launching-online-this-fall). Once your own parent company sells a cola with your main ingredient, that ingredient no longer sets you apart.

So the gut health promise was exposed from two sides at once. Customers could question whether it worked, and rivals could put the same fiber in their own cans, which means a brand built on that benefit has to defend it in court and on the shelf.

## Liquid Death's marketing strategy let the can do the talking

Liquid Death started from the opposite end. Beer had all the cool packaging and water had none, so founder Mike Cessario put water in a tallboy can that looks like beer. "There's no reason that only beer can have that kind of cool look and feel," he told [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/03/15/liquid-death-ceo-mike-cessario-interview-beverage-unicorn-water-beer) in 2024. "We needed to bake all the marketing and shareability into the product itself."

Because the can did the selling, the brand never had to claim its water was special. [Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/brand-marketing/liquid-deaths-first-super-bowl-ad-says-its-ok-to-drink-on-the-job/) called "Don't be scared, it's just water" the brand's long-running tagline when Liquid Death ran its first national Super Bowl ad in 2025. The line admits the product is ordinary and makes the brand itself the reason to buy.

When Liquid Death does make a claim, it prints a number you can check. Its sodas carry "90% less sugar than the leading soda" on the can, Modern Retail reported, and its energy drink label reads "Unextreme Caffeine (100mg) = 1 Coffee," according to [its product page](https://liquiddeath.com/products/sparkling-energy-vp1). The approach has paid off: Liquid Death reached an all-time high of $333 million in retail sales in 2024, Modern Retail reported, and Fortune put its valuation at $1.4 billion in 2024.

**Steal this:** put the personality on the package and the claims in numbers, so the fun part does the selling and the factual part never needs defending. Graza made a similar bet when it [put olive oil in a squeeze bottle](/blog/behind-the-strategy-graza).

## Poppi is already selling what Liquid Death sells

The usual read on this matchup is that Poppi won: it sold for $1.95 billion, while Liquid Death is a joke brand that sells water. I read it the other way. Poppi's gut health promise got it onto the shelf, but the brand PepsiCo markets today looks a lot more like Liquid Death than like the Poppi of 2021.

The shift started before the sale. At a May 2024 industry event, Ellsworth said most people drink Poppi for the flavor rather than for functional benefits, and suggested the brand would keep pushing that message, Brewbound reported. Its 2026 Super Bowl ad, "Make it poppi," starred Charli xcx and Rachel Sennott turning a dull lecture hall into a party, and PepsiCo's [description of the ad](https://www.pepsico.com/newsroom/stories/2026/poppi-debuts-super-bowl-lx-ad-featuring-charli-xcx-and-rachel-sennott) never mentions gut health or prebiotics.

In October 2026, Poppi signed an official sponsorship with the Dallas Cowboys built around the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders, pouring rights at AT&T Stadium, and social content, [Marketing Brew reported](https://www.marketingbrew.com/stories/poppi-dallas-cowboys-cheerleaders). SVP of marketing Kristina MacIntosh told the outlet that the brand sits "within those sidelines of culture" and that awareness and trial are the goals for the deal. That is a brand selling a mood and a crowd to belong to, which is what Liquid Death has sold from the start.

So the toilet ad mocked the one thing Poppi was already backing away from. Liquid Death didn't have to change its story when the soda aisle got crowded, because its story never depended on what was in the can.

## What to check before you put a benefit in your tagline

Poppi's run shows that a functional benefit can earn trial fast, and Liquid Death's shows what keeps working after the copycats arrive. If you market a product with a benefit, I'd put the claim through these checks before building a campaign on it:

- **Could you prove it if a customer sued?** If the answer depends on a study you don't have, say less, or say it as a number on the label.
- **Could a bigger company copy it next quarter?** If it's an ingredient, assume yes, and decide what will still set you apart when they do.
- **Would people buy for the brand alone?** Build that part while the claim is doing its job, whether it's a voice like Liquid Death's or a ritual like the one [Ghia built around the hour before dinner](/blog/behind-the-strategy-ghia).

The test I'd run next is simple: cut the benefit out of your ad and see whether people still stop scrolling. If they do, the brand can stand on its own, and the claim becomes a bonus instead of the thing you have to defend.

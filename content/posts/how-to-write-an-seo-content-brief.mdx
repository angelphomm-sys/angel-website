Have you ever opened a blank page, written three paragraphs, and then realized you didn't know who the post was for?

An SEO content brief fixes that before you start. It's a one-page plan for a single blog post: who it's for, what it should do, which Google search it should answer, and what makes it different from everything that already shows up. I write one before every post on this blog, and the video below shows me building one from scratch.

This guide is for people who write their own posts: marketers starting out, freelancers, small business owners, and anyone building a personal brand. Most guides on this topic are written for agency teams handing a brief to a writer. There's a free template to download near the end.

## What is an SEO content brief?

It's a plan for one post, built around what people search. The "SEO" part, short for search engine optimization, just means you start with how your reader looks for the topic on Google.

It isn't the same as a **creative brief**, which plans a campaign or design project like an ad or a rebrand. The two get mixed up, even in Google's results.

## What's changed in SEO, and what hasn't

The biggest change you'll notice is the AI summary at the top of many Google searches, called an AI Overview.

<Figure src="/images/blog/how-to-write-an-seo-content-brief/google-ai-overview-seo-content-brief.jpg" alt="Google search results for seo content brief, with an AI Overview at the top defining the term and listing the core elements of a strong brief" ratio="5/3" />

The basics haven't changed, according to Google itself. Its guide to [AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) says there are "no additional requirements" to appear in AI Overviews, and that the same core SEO practices apply. Its guide to [helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks whether your content shows "first-hand expertise," like having actually used the product or done the work. The same guide answers a common myth directly: Google doesn't have a preferred word count.

For your brief, that means two things. Plan the basics the same way, and plan what only you can add, because that's what a summary can't copy.

## How to write an SEO content brief in 7 steps

### 1. Define your target audience and goal

Write one sentence about who you're writing for (your target audience) and one about what they should know or be able to do when they finish (your content goal). Every other choice gets checked against these two lines.

### 2. Choose your primary keyword and check search intent

Your primary keyword is the main phrase your audience types into Google. Find it, plus two or three secondary keywords: related phrases the post should also cover. I use Ahrefs, but the free [Ahrefs Keyword Generator](https://ahrefs.com/keyword-generator), Google's autocomplete, and the "People also ask" box all work. Note each phrase's monthly search volume and keyword difficulty if your tool shows them.

Then search the keyword yourself and look at what kind of page shows up most: a how-to, a list, a review. That's the search intent: what people want when they search. Semrush lists [focusing on a keyword without checking this](https://www.semrush.com/blog/seo-content-brief/) as a common challenge when writing briefs.

### 3. Analyze the top three results and find the content gap

Open the top three results that aren't ads. Note their headings, roughly how long they are, and what they skip. If Google shows an AI Overview, note what it covers and what it leaves out. What they all miss is your content gap, and it's where your post earns its place.

### 4. Decide your editorial angle and unique value

Your editorial angle is the one idea your post adds, in one sentence. Your unique value is what only you can add: a story, a photo, a result, or an example from your own work.

For length, I average the top three results and divide by three. It's a starting point, not a rule. RankUp's beginner guide even recommends [leaving fixed word counts out of a brief](https://www.rankup.so/academy/seo-content-briefs). Cover the topic fully and cut what doesn't help the reader.

### 5. Outline from the questions people ask

Turn the questions from step 2 into your headings. Big questions become H2 sections, follow-up questions become H3 subsections, and anything left over can go in a short FAQ at the end. Give each section a rough word count so the length stays on track.

Then write your SEO title and meta description, the short summary that shows under your title on Google. Put the main keyword near the start of both.

### 6. Check your sources before you write

List every source, photo, and link you plan to use, then open each one and make sure it still says what you need. While planning my Owala vs. Stanley post, I found that a quote I wanted to use was no longer on Owala's own site, so it came out before it ever reached a draft.

### 7. Choose your call to action and KPI

Decide what you want the reader to do after reading: your call to action (CTA). Then pick one KPI, short for key performance indicator: a number to check 30 days after publishing, like your Google ranking or clicks to another page.

## What I'm still improving

My weakest step is the last one. On my [Utah Mammoth post](/blog/utah-mammoth-name), I rewrote the ending three times because I hadn't decided what I wanted readers to take away. If I had written the next step into the brief, the ending would have been settled before the draft. I also used to skip the success number entirely, which made it hard to tell later whether a post did its job.

## Try it on your next post

Search changes every year, but the fundamentals in this brief haven't: know your audience, answer what they're searching for, add something only you can, and check your facts. Download the template, fill it in before your next post, and notice how much faster the first draft comes when the decisions are already made.

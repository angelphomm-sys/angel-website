Have you ever opened a blank page, written three paragraphs, and then realized you didn't know who the post was for?

A content brief fixes that before you start. It's a one-page plan for a single piece of content: who it's for, what it should do, which search it should answer, and what makes it different from everything already ranking. I write one before every post on this blog, and the video below shows me building one from scratch.

Most guides on this topic are written for teams handing a brief to a writer. This one is for the person who writes the brief and the post, which is how many marketers, freelancers, and small business owners work. There's a free template to download near the end.

## Content brief vs. creative brief

The two names get mixed up, even in search results. A **content brief** plans one piece of content, usually with search in mind. A **creative brief** plans a campaign or design project, like an ad or a rebrand. Everything below is about the first one.

## How to write a content brief in 7 steps

### 1. Start with the reader and the goal

Write one sentence about who you're writing for and one about what they should know or do when they finish. Every later decision, from the keyword to the call to action, gets checked against these two lines.

### 2. Pick your keyword and check search intent

Find the phrase your reader actually searches, plus two or three related ones. I use Ahrefs, but the free [Ahrefs Keyword Generator](https://ahrefs.com/keyword-generator), Google's autocomplete, and the "People also ask" box all work.

Then search the phrase yourself and look at what ranks. That tells you what people expect to find. When I searched "how to write a content brief," half the results were about creative briefs, which is why this post explains the difference up front.

### 3. Study the top three results and find the gap

Open the top three results that aren't ads. Note their headings, roughly how long they are, and what they skip. The gap is where your post earns its place: a missing example, an audience nobody writes for, or a question nobody answers.

### 4. Set the angle and a starting length

Your angle is the one idea your post adds. For length, I average the top three word counts and divide by three. It's a starting point, not a quota. Cover the topic fully, and cut anything that doesn't serve the reader.

### 5. Outline from the questions people ask

Turn the questions from step 2 into your headings. Broad questions become H2s, follow-ups become H3s, and anything left over can go in a short FAQ.

### 6. List your sources and verify them first

Write down every source you plan to use, plus images and internal links. Then open each one and confirm it still says what you think. While writing my Owala vs. Stanley post, I found that a quote I planned to use was no longer on Owala's own site, so it came out before it ever reached a draft.

### 7. Decide the call to action and how you'll measure it

Choose what you want the reader to do next, and one number that would tell you the post worked, like rankings, time on the page, or clicks to another page.

## What I'm still improving

My weakest step is the last one. On my Utah Mammoth post, I rewrote the ending three times because I hadn't decided what I wanted readers to take away. If I had written the call to action in the brief, the ending would have been settled before the draft. I also used to skip the success measure entirely, which made it hard to tell later whether a post did its job.

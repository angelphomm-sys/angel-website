I grew up in Warroad, Minnesota, in an immigrant family where everyone, including me, worked at the local window factory. I went in when it was dark and came out when it was dark. One day a coworker told me, "Get out of here before it's too late."

I didn't know yet what "out" looked like. That sentence followed me to Utah, into a college application no one at home could help me fill out, and through a first semester I almost didn't finish. This is the story of how I stayed, and of the three things that got me through: knowing why I started, finding people who had done it before, and learning to ask why. If you're the first in your family to try this, I hope it saves you some of the guessing.

## Starting without a map

Warroad sits five miles from the Canadian border, the kind of place where most people never leave. As the oldest child, I helped take care of my family before I ever thought about what I wanted. My coworker's words stayed with me anyway. When a friend offered me a room in Utah, I packed everything I owned into my car and drove 20 hours to build something of my own.

College was not the plan. A close friend suggested I go "to figure it out," and I took the advice. No one at home had done it before, so I learned the application and the FAFSA from scratch while working full time, and paid my way with scholarships and grants. I was a few years older than most of my classmates and still learning things they seemed to already know.

## The semester I almost quit

My first semester, I felt out of place. My grades weren't where I wanted them, and there were moments I wanted to give up entirely. I didn't know how to network or where to find help, and asking felt harder than doing the work.

So I stopped and asked myself why I started. I had spent years putting my family first, and college was the first thing I did just for me. Remembering that didn't make the work easier. It made the next step clear: I applied for scholarships and got involved on campus.

## Finding the people who had done it

That decision carried me into my second semester, when I found Hope Scholars, a new scholarship program at my business school, and joined its first group in 2024. Walking in was intimidating, but the mentors and students there knew what it felt like to do college without a roadmap.

Hope Scholars, TRIO, and the First Generation Center became my community. My mentors listened when I needed to talk and encouraged me when I doubted myself. I still had to do the work. I just didn't have to do it alone anymore.

## Learning to ask why

For years, I stayed quiet and followed whoever sounded most confident, because I assumed they knew more than I did. College changed that. My professors pushed me to question things, and I learned to ask why.

That habit led me to digital marketing, where my creativity and my business ambitions finally fit together. At Yoodlize, a startup, I ran YouTube ad tests before launch, and the winning video brought people to our site at 31 percent lower cost. The test told me which video won. It couldn't tell me why. That question is the part of marketing I want to keep learning, and it started the day I gave myself permission to ask.

## Building the help I once needed

Once I had a community, I wanted to build one for the students after me. Hope Scholars had no one running its marketing, so I proposed a role that didn't exist yet, and the program said yes. Now I help tell its story to donors, from the website to the events where I speak. Since I took on the role, the program has grown from 17 students to 45, and I've turned some of our monthly meetings into workshops that draw about 35 students each.

None of that was handed to me. But I didn't have to figure it out alone.

## What I'd tell anyone starting over

Your path may look different. These are the things that made the difference for me.

- **Go to the office built for you.** TRIO or your school's first-gen center. Walk in and ask what they help with.
- **Find one person who has done it.** One is enough to start.
- **Apply for the scholarships that fit your story.** You'll write a stronger essay when you can say why it fits.
- **Ask why, out loud.** The confident person in the room doesn't always know more than you.
- **Get to know the people next to you.** The person beside you in class could open your next door.
- **Save your proof.** Every project and result becomes your résumé, and your confidence.

## What education means to me

One day, after a career in marketing, I want to teach the way my professors taught me and help students learn to ask why for themselves.

bell hooks calls education "the practice of freedom." That's what I was after. Not just a degree or a better job, but the freedom to think for myself, to question the path I was handed, and to decide who I get to become.

If you're first-gen too, who was the first person who helped you figure it out?

After high school, I worked at the window factory in Warroad, Minnesota. I went in when it was dark and came out when it was dark. One day a coworker told me, "Get out of here before it's too late."

Everyone in college is figuring out who they are and what they want to do. As the first in my family to go, I had to figure it out without anyone at home who had done it before. Three things got me through: knowing why I wanted a degree, finding a community I could rely on, and doing the work myself. I'm sharing what I learned in hopes it helps you find your way too.

## Starting without a map

Warroad is a small town five miles from the Canadian border, the kind of place where most people never leave. My coworker's words stuck with me. I didn't want to wake up years later regretting dreams I never tried to chase. So I saved up, packed everything I owned into my car, and drove 20 hours through Wyoming to start over in Utah.

College was not the plan. A close friend suggested I go "to figure it out," and I took the advice. Then I did the rest on my own. I filled out the application on my own. I figured out the FAFSA, the federal form that decides your financial aid, on my own. I did it while working full time and paying my own bills.

I was a few years older than most of my classmates, and still learning things they seemed to already know. That gap is common. Half of first-generation college students complete an internship, compared with about two-thirds of their peers, according to [Handshake data reported by the American Council on Education in 2025](https://www.higheredtoday.org/2025/03/03/the-changing-landscape-of-internships-in-higher-education/).

## The semester I almost quit

Being somewhere unfamiliar was overwhelming. My first semester, I felt out of place, and I wasn't sure I was cut out for this. My grades weren't where I wanted them, and there were moments I wanted to give up entirely.

Reaching out didn't come naturally. It wasn't that I didn't want help. Asking for it felt harder than doing the work.

So I stopped and asked myself why I started.

I've always put other people first. College was the first thing I did just for me. I wanted a chance at the future I had dreamed about.

Once I knew that, the next step still wasn't easy, but it was clear. With encouragement from that same friend, I applied for scholarships and started getting involved on campus.

## Finding the people who had done it

That decision carried me into my second semester, and that's when I found Hope Scholars, a scholarship and mentorship program for first-generation students at Utah Valley University. I joined its first group in 2024. Walking in the first time was intimidating. I was welcomed right away by mentors and students who knew what it felt like to do college without a roadmap.

Hope Scholars, TRIO (a federal program that gives first-gen students advising and support), and the First Generation Center became my community. My mentors listened when I needed to talk, encouraged me when I doubted myself, and reminded me I didn't have to do this alone.

## Still my work

They guided me. They didn't do it for me.

Hope Scholars was raising money from donors but had no Instagram and no plan for what to post. I wrote a proposal for a marketing internship that didn't exist yet, pitched it to the program directors, and they said yes. I built the Instagram account from nothing, decided what we would post about, and made the materials for donors. I spoke at donor events and board meetings to raise money for students like me. I studied abroad in Italy.

None of that was handed to me. But I didn't have to figure it out alone.

## What I'd tell anyone starting over

Your path may look different. These are the things that made the difference for me.

- **Go to the office built for you.** TRIO or your school's first-gen center. Walk in and ask, "What do you help with?"
- **Find one person who has done it.** A professor, an advisor, someone a couple of years ahead of you. One is enough to start.
- **Find the scholarships that fit you.** Start close to home: your school's scholarship page, your department or college, your first-gen or TRIO office, and local community foundations. Apply to the ones that match your story and goals. You'll write a stronger essay when you can say why it fits.
- **Get involved on campus, and get to know the people next to you.** The person beside you in class could be a future colleague or the connection that opens a door. Networking starts in the classroom, not at formal events.
- **Save your proof.** Every project, pitch, and result. It becomes your résumé, and your confidence.

## What education means to me

bell hooks calls education "the practice of freedom." That's what I was after. Not just a degree or a better job, but the freedom to think for myself, to question the path I was handed, and to decide who I get to become.

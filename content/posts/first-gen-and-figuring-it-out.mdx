I grew up in Warroad, Minnesota, in an immigrant family where everyone, including me, worked at the local window factory. I went in when it was dark and came out when it was dark. One day a coworker told me, "Get out of here before it's too late."

I didn't know yet what "out" looked like. That sentence followed me to Utah, into a college application no one at home could help me fill out, and through a first semester I almost didn't finish. This is the story of how I stayed, and of the three things that got me through: knowing why I started, finding people who had done it before, and learning to ask why. If you're the first in your family to try this, I hope it saves you some of the guessing.

## Starting without a map

Warroad sits five miles from the Canadian border, the kind of place where most people never leave. As the oldest child, I helped take care of my family before I ever thought about what I wanted. My coworker's words stayed with me anyway. When a friend offered me a room in Utah, I packed everything I owned into my car and drove 20 hours through Wyoming to build something of my own.

College was not the plan. A close friend suggested I go "to figure it out," and I took the advice, even though no one in my family had done it before. There was no one at home to walk me through the application or the FAFSA, the federal form that decides your financial aid, so I learned both from scratch while working full time. Scholarships and federal grants paid my way.

I was a few years older than most of my classmates, and still learning things they seemed to already know. That gap is common. Half of first-generation college students complete an internship, compared with about two-thirds of their peers, according to [Handshake data reported by the American Council on Education in 2025](https://www.higheredtoday.org/2025/03/03/the-changing-landscape-of-internships-in-higher-education/).

## The semester I almost quit

My first semester, I felt out of place, and I wasn't sure I was cut out for this. My grades weren't where I wanted them, and there were moments I wanted to give up entirely.

Asking for help didn't come naturally. I didn't know how to network, or where to find internships and scholarships, and asking felt harder than doing the work.

So I stopped and asked myself why I started. I had spent years putting my family first. College was the first thing I did just for me, and I wanted a chance at the future I had dreamed about. Once I knew that, the next step still wasn't easy, but it was clear. I applied for scholarships and started getting involved on campus.

## Finding the people who had done it

That decision carried me into my second semester, and that's when I found Hope Scholars, a program at Utah Valley University's Woodbury School of Business that prepares students for graduation and a career. I joined its first group in 2024. Walking in the first time was intimidating, but I was welcomed right away by mentors and students who knew what it felt like to do college without a roadmap.

Hope Scholars, TRIO (a federal program that gives first-gen students advising and support), and the First Generation Center became my community. My mentors listened when I needed to talk, encouraged me when I doubted myself, and reminded me I didn't have to do this alone.

## Learning to ask why

For years, I stayed quiet and followed whoever sounded most confident, because I assumed they knew more than I did. College changed that. My professors pushed me to question things instead of accepting them, and I learned to ask why.

That habit led me to digital marketing, where my creativity and my business ambitions finally worked together. It also follows me into every project. As an intern at Yoodlize, a rental app startup, I ran YouTube ad tests before our launch, and the winning video brought people to our website at 31 percent lower cost. The test told me which video won. It couldn't tell me why. That question, what makes people choose one thing over another, is the part of marketing I want to keep learning.

## Building the help I once needed

My mentors guided me. They didn't do it for me, and now I try to do the same for the students after me.

Hope Scholars had no one running its marketing, so I proposed a marketing role that didn't exist yet, and the program said yes. Now I run its marketing alongside the director and the assistant dean. I designed the website, refined the brand message, and created the brochures and campaigns that reach our donors. I speak at donor events, and together those efforts have helped raise hundreds of thousands of dollars. Since I took on the role, the program has grown from 17 students to 45, and it can now fund up to 80 scholarships.

I also turned three of our monthly meetings into workshops. I planned the topics and speakers with input from the director and students, and about 35 students joined each one. I used their feedback to shape the next.

None of that was handed to me. But I didn't have to figure it out alone.

## What I'd tell anyone starting over

Your path may look different. These are the things that made the difference for me.

- **Go to the office built for you.** TRIO or your school's first-gen center. Walk in and ask, "What do you help with?"
- **Find one person who has done it.** A professor, an advisor, someone a couple of years ahead of you. One is enough to start.
- **Find the scholarships that fit you.** Start close to home: your school's scholarship page, your department or college, your first-gen or TRIO office, and local community foundations. You'll write a stronger essay when you can say why it fits.
- **Ask why, out loud.** The confident person in the room doesn't always know more than you. Your question is often the one everyone else is holding back.
- **Get involved on campus, and get to know the people next to you.** The person beside you in class could be a future colleague or the connection that opens a door.
- **Save your proof.** Every project, pitch, and result. It becomes your résumé, and your confidence.

## What education means to me

One day, after a career in marketing, I want to teach the way my professors taught me and help students learn to ask why for themselves.

bell hooks calls education "the practice of freedom." That's what I was after. Not just a degree or a better job, but the freedom to think for myself, to question the path I was handed, and to decide who I get to become.

If you're first-gen too, who was the first person who helped you figure it out?

Bloom Nutrition's marketing strategy runs on creators: 5,600 of them since 2021, by co-founder Mari Llewellyn's count. That program already pays for reach, so Bloom's pop-ups need a different job. I think that job is capture, turning the people who show up into a list Bloom owns, and Bloom's own events show why.

**The short version**

- Bloom's reach comes from a creator program that takes about 75 percent of its marketing budget.
- Its 2023 NYC pop-up recap is strong content but gives viewers nowhere to go next.
- A later pop-up added an on-site sign-up, and 5,500 people joined Bloom's community on the TYB app in the first month.

## What is Bloom Nutrition's marketing strategy?

Bloom's marketing started with one creator's audience. Llewellyn posted a before-and-after photo on Instagram in 2017 when she had 900 followers, and within a year she had more than 400,000, she said in an as-told-to essay for [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/sydney-sladovnik/how-an-army-of-tiktokers-drove-12-billion-video-views-for-my-company.html) in August 2024. She launched Bloom with her husband, Greg LaVecchia, in 2019, and by then, in her words, "my social media community already felt very much a part of the brand."

Then Bloom borrowed other creators' audiences. It set up an in-house influencer team, separate from its social media team, and since 2021 that team has worked with 5,600 creators whose Bloom-related videos have drawn more than 12 billion views. Llewellyn credits the creative freedom those creators got: "It wasn't so much a strategy as it was letting influencers be themselves." The program costs about 75 percent of Bloom's marketing budget, and she tied it to nearly $170 million in revenue in 2023.

[Graza](/blog/behind-the-strategy-graza) started from the other side, seeding food creators with free oil and asking for no posts. Bloom's founder was the creator, so a creator program extended what already worked. That budget split also sets the terms for everything else: when three quarters of the budget already buys reach, every other channel, events included, has to earn its place some other way.

## Why does Bloom's pop-up recap work as content?

The set does most of the work. Bloom built a corner market where a greens powder sits on shelves like fruit, and "Find your flavor" turns a supplement into something you browse. Every station is a shot a guest could take on their own phone, so the people in the room film the ad for Bloom.

The thank-you framing helps too. For people who came, the recap is a keepsake, and for people who did not, it is proof they missed something. Saves outnumber comments 68 to 11, which suggests viewers filed the clip away instead of joining a conversation. As event content, it works. The harder question is whether event content should be a Bloom pop-up's main job at all.

## Can a pop-up compete with Bloom's creators on reach?

At Bloom's size, it cannot, and that is the point I want to argue. The recap has 26,100 views, while the creator program has drawn more than 12 billion, so even a well-built set barely registers next to the channel Bloom already pays for.

Bloom's own leadership does not plan events around impressions math either. In April 2026, Bloom wrapped a 7-Eleven near the Palm Springs airport in pink for Coachella weekend, with DJs, samples, and an airbrush merch station, to promote its energy drinks' nationwide rollout at 7-Eleven, [Modern Retail reported](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/bloom-pacsun-and-more-chase-coachella-crowds-on-the-way-to-the-show-with-roadside-activations/) that month. CEO Greg LaVecchia said an event gets his approval when it works both online and in person, and that the team is not "reverse engineering the whole program for how many impressions we're going to get at what cost."

Modern Retail also reported that Bloom would judge the takeover mainly on awareness, social chatter, and virality, and that it was the second of five or six large activations planned for 2026. That scorecard is where I disagree. Chatter is what Bloom already buys at scale from creators, while a pop-up offers something no feed can: a person standing in front of you. I would score Bloom's events on how many of those people leave as contacts Bloom can reach again, because a creator's audience is rented and a guest list is owned.

## What did the Treat Shoppe pop-up do differently?

Bloom tested a version of this at a later New York pop-up, the Treat Shoppe. It launched its community on TYB, a fan rewards app, the same week and set up an on-site sign-up booth so guests could join before they left. TYB's [case study](https://www.tyb.xyz/case-studies/bloom-activates-community-at-exclusive-irl-experiences-driving-68-growth-during-treat-shoppe-week) reports more than 250 RSVPs to the event, 5,500 people joining Bloom's TYB community in the first month, and 13,000 new email subscribers.

Jillian Reulein, Bloom's head of social and content, said in the case study that the launch let the team "capture that initial excitement and turn an in-person moment into an ongoing relationship." That is the job description I would give every Bloom pop-up.

Read those numbers with care, because TYB is a vendor reporting on its own client. Its claim that members order 73 percent more often than non-members compares people who chose to join with people who did not, so it shows Bloom found its loyal fans more than it proves the app made them loyal. The structure is still the lesson: guests got a reason and a place to leave their name.

**Steal this:** Put a staffed sign-up at the exit of your set, with one reason to join that only works for people who were there.

## What I would change in the 2023 recap

I would lead with the people. Guests appear throughout, but the captions name objects for seven cuts and save the community for the eighth, so the recap reads as a store tour with a crowd at the end. Open on the groups posing by the Greens wall, and the same footage reads as a party.

I would test that call instead of guessing. At Yoodlize, I split-tested two YouTube thumbnails, a party-first one against an app-first one, and let click-through pick the winner. Here that means a people-first cut and a set-first cut of the same footage, then keeping whichever viewers save and share more.

I would also give the viewer a next step, because the recap ends with nowhere to go and has 11 comments. A line like "tag yourself if you came" turns attendees into commenters and gives Bloom their handles, the TikTok version of the Treat Shoppe's sign-up booth. The set did include a "Find Your Flavor" quiz, but the recap does not show whether it collected anything, so I can't count it as capture.

## A pop-up checklist from Bloom's playbook

- **Build the set as a backdrop.** Every station should be a shot a guest would take on their own phone, like Bloom's Greens wall.
- **Hand out one branded prop.** Bloom's custom bags and baskets carried the logo into guests' photos.
- **Cut the recap people-first.** Put guests on camera before the product, then test it against a set-first cut. For more crowd-first activations, see the [best brand activations of 2026](/blog/10-brand-moments-people-showed-up-for-2026).
- **Collect contacts before people leave.** A staffed sign-up, like the Treat Shoppe's TYB booth, turns a visit into a relationship you can count.

## What to watch at Bloom's Club Apple pop-up

Bloom's next test is Club Apple, a SoHo pop-up for its Apple Edit on October 24 and 25, 2026, which promises unreleased flavor tastings, a portrait studio, DJ sets, and a vote on which unreleased flavor should launch next, per the [NYC for FREE event listing](https://www.nycforfree.co/events/bloom-nutrition-club-apple).

The flavor vote is the most useful idea there, because it gives every guest a reason to leave a name and a preference. What I want to know is whether the vote runs through a sign-up or a sticker wall, since only one of those leaves Bloom with a list. At your next event, try the same move: give guests one choice that only counts when they sign up, then count the sign-ups before you count the views.
